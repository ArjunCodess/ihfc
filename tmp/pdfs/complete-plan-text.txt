 Build two differential-drive robots around the team's proposed architecture. CareBot carries the medical kits in tilted
 chutes and collects the coloured patient cylinders through a front lift. It reads each patient's colour at the top, routes the
 patient into a destination bin, and later empties that bin with a servo gate. SealBot carries both wooden beams in low
 grippers under a raised loader flap. It stages the beams first without closing the quarantine perimeter, then lowers the
 same flap to collect the three sample discs, stores them in a covered lane, and releases them one at a time at the
 laboratory. A final low push closes the staged beam after sample delivery.

 This revision locks that architecture. It removes the earlier fixed patient pocket, direct patient delivery, separate sample
 tray, and camera-under-tray arrangement. Dimensions below are prototype starting values, not finished CAD. The real
 game pieces, start gauge, laboratory, and field markings decide the final clearances.

 CareBot needs four patient bins, not three. Red goes to the hospital bin, green goes to the recovery bin, and alternating
 yellow patients go to PCC-A and PCC-B. This preserves the optional 2-2 yellow bonus without asking one outlet to
 count two pieces and retain two.

 The replacement for the proposed caterpillar mechanism is a gravity magazine. Once the input flap lifts a disc above
 the internal lane, a small slope carries it to the rear. A linked two-stop escapement blocks the queue while the front disc
 drops. This has fewer moving parts than a track, does not depend on a special tread, and still gives the requested front
 input, internal transport, and rear output sequence.

 3. CareBot mechanical layout
 CareBot has three levels. The lower front contains the patient intake and roller lift. The middle level holds the colour cell
 and four patient bins. The upper or rear level holds the three kit chutes. Keep all outlets visible and reachable for
 clearing jams, and use removable transparent covers over every loaded lane.

 Patient pickup without caterpillar wheels
 Use a 50-wide V-shaped mouth that narrows to a 22-24-wide vertical channel. A 0.3-0.5 PET lip starts about 0.5 above
 the board and slides under the lower edge of the upright patient. Two stacked pairs of rubber-faced rollers lift the
 cylinder. Start with 35-45-diameter light wheels, with the left and right rollers turning inward and upward.

 The roller gap should compress the patient gently, initially 18-19 for a measured 20-diameter cylinder. Put one side on
 a spring-loaded plate so paint thickness and small diameter differences do not cause a jam. Cover wooden or plastic
 rollers with silicone tube, foam tape, or wide rubber bands. One small geared DC motor can drive all four rollers
 through crossed rubber-band loops or simple gears. No commercial track is required.

 Add a back plate behind the rollers and 22-high side guides so the patient cannot escape or turn sideways. The lift only
 handles one patient at a time. A lower presence switch starts the lift, and an upper switch or break-beam stops it when
 the cylinder reaches the colour cell. If the upper sensor does not trigger within the tested lift time, reverse the rollers
 slowly and stop that pickup routine.

 Colour-reading cell
 The patient enters a matte black inspection cell at the top of the lift and rests on a closed floor gate. Mount a
 TCS34725-type RGB sensor 15-25 from the painted surface with diffused white LEDs beside it. A short hood blocks
 field lighting. Read only while the drive motors and elevator are stopped.

 Calibrate using all twelve real patients. Save red, yellow, and green ranges from repeated readings under the expected
 lights. Require several agreeing samples and keep an UNKNOWN result. If the reading is unknown, hold the patient,
 read once more, then stop the patient routine rather than putting it in the wrong bin.

 Four-way sorting and storage
 Below the colour cell, use one servo-driven selector chute with four fixed positions. The positions feed H-red,
 RZ-green, PCC-A-yellow, and PCC-B-yellow bins. After the selector reaches its mechanical stop, a second servo
 opens the inspection-cell floor so the patient falls into that bin. Close the floor before moving the selector again.

 Alternate accepted yellow patients between PCC-A and PCC-B. Each yellow bin holds two patients. The red and green
 bins each hold four. Make the patient lanes about 23-24 internal width and 23-25 internal height, with red and green
 usable lane length near 95 and each yellow lane near 52. Start with a 15-degree decline toward the outlet. The exact
 angle comes from a slide test with the painted pieces.

 Each bin has its own SG90-operated outlet gate and a fixed stop that carries the load while the servo is idle. A flexible
 apron ending 10-15 above the field reduces bouncing. The outlet must enter the destination boundary before opening.
 CareBot can creep backward 40-70 during a batch release if stationary tests show that pieces pile up at the gate.

 Medical-kit chutes
 The kits do not pass through the roller lift or colour cell. Load them before the match into three separate covered
 chutes: six for H, two for PCC-A, and two for PCC-B. Start with 29 internal width, 23 internal height, and 165 usable
 length for all three lanes. A 30-35-degree slope is a reasonable first prototype, but the shallowest angle that passes
 repeated slide tests is better for stability.

 Each kit chute has one independent outlet servo. A mechanical stop and light elastic keep the gate shut without servo
 torque. Keep kit and patient outlets physically separated so an earlier delivery does not block the later one. Label every
 lane on the robot because loading the wrong 2-piece chute loses the distribution bonus even if the mechanism works.

 4. SealBot mechanical layout
 SealBot uses one large front input flap, one fixed rear output assembly, two beam grippers under the raised flap, and a
 single-file disc magazine through the centre. The input flap stays locked in its upper position while the beams are
 carried. Once both beams are staged and released, the flap becomes the sample loader.

 Two upright beam grippers under the flap
 Mount two low gripper bays below and just behind the raised input flap. At the start, each bay holds one beam at its
 centre while the beam stands on its 20-wide lower face. The gripper keeps the beam upright and controls yaw; it does
 not lift the beam from the board.

 Each bay has a fixed back pad and a servo-operated wraparound finger. Set the closed throat to the measured beam
 thickness plus about 0.5, with light foam compression. Set the open throat to at least 27 so the robot can reverse




CareBot / SealBot | Locked mechanism architecture | 6 September 2026                                                          4
 without dragging the released beam. Put the contact height near 8-12 above the field. A hard stop, rather than servo
 stall torque, resists travel forces.

 Carry both beams slowly, with their long axes parallel at the start. Release the first beam in its final wall-contact pose.
 Put the second beam on its black line but leave a doorway wider than SealBot's loaded body, initially the body width
 plus 30. Open the second gripper and withdraw. Both grippers are now clear, so the input flap can lower for the sample
 mission.

 After all three samples leave quarantine and reach the laboratory, approach the staged second beam from outside the
 future enclosure. Use a fixed padded bumper only 8-10 above the field to slide that beam lengthwise through the
 remaining gap until it touches the required wall and the first beam. The push must follow the beam's long axis; a
 sideways push is likely to tip it. Then reverse completely. Final success still depends on both beams touching the
 required walls, touching each other, covering the marked line, standing independently, and clearing the robot.

 Releasing both beams in their final positions before sample collection is not a valid default sequence. The field drawing
 shows those beams completing the quarantine perimeter, so early closure can block SealBot from reaching the discs or
 the laboratory. If a full-size field test proves a clear route despite closure, the final push can be removed; until then,
 stage first and close last.

 The carried beams define SealBot's true swept envelope. Avoid in-place turns while either beam is attached. Start
 beam travel near 40-50 mm/s with low acceleration and increase speed only after repeated no-tip tests.

 JCB-style disc input flap
 Make the input flap a shallow 68-72-wide bucket with 10-15-high cheeks and a 0.3 PET leading lip. A metal-gear servo
 drives two equal side links so the flap lowers nearly flat for pickup, rises without twisting, and tips inward at the top. This
 is the requested JCB motion, but a four-bar linkage keeps the two sides together better than a single unsupported
 hinge.

 In the pickup position, the lip is 0.3-0.5 above the field. SealBot approaches a centred disc at about 20 mm/s until the
 disc lies fully on the bucket. A presence switch or reflected-light sensor confirms capture. The flap then rises and tips
 20-30 degrees inward so the disc slides into the covered magazine. Side cheeks and a roof gap of about 6.5 prevent
 the 5-thick disc from escaping or riding over another disc.

 Fit upper and lower physical stops. The servo reaches the stop through a linkage with a little compliance, then relaxes.
 If the disc does not clear the flap at the upper position, do not shake the whole robot; lower once, reseat, and retry
 once.

 Gravity magazine instead of a caterpillar
 Use a straight covered lane through the robot, about 60.5-61 internal width and at least 180 usable length for three
 56-diameter discs. Keep the roof 6.5-7 above the floor. Give the lane a 5-8-degree fall from the input toward the rear
 output. Smooth PET on the floor provides a low-friction surface.

 A single-servo escapement replaces the missing caterpillar mechanism. The servo links an upstream holding pin to the
 rear output flap. In the REST position, the rear flap holds the first disc and the upstream pin is raised. In the RELEASE
 position, the upstream pin drops between the first and second discs before the rear flap opens. Only the first disc can
 leave. When the servo returns, the rear flap closes first, then the upstream pin rises so the next disc slides forward.

 Space the two stops from tests with actual 56 discs. Make the linkage sequence mechanical, so a single servo
 command cannot open both paths at once. Add a small window or top sensor at the front of the queue to confirm that a
 disc advanced after each cycle. The magazine must retain all discs when power is removed.

 Rear output and laboratory alignment
 The output is a fixed short chute at the rear, ending 3-6 above the laboratory surface. Its internal opening is 58-59 wide
 and centred on the robot. A short flexible apron prevents the disc from gaining sideways speed as it falls.

 Use a three-channel or five-channel downward IR line array around the output, not a single colour sensor. One colour
 reading can detect dark versus light but cannot tell whether a 56 disc is centred in a 60 slot. The left and right sensors
 provide an error direction, while the centre sensor confirms the slot or opening is beneath the outlet. Calibrate the array
 on the actual laboratory surface because a painted circle, a cut hole, and a shadow return different values.

 Approach each measured slot using wheel odometry and field lines, then stop for local sensing. Correct heading first.
 Make short left or right wheel movements until the two side readings are symmetric and the centre reading matches the
 stored slot signature. Release one disc without moving the chassis. A second sensor in the chute confirms that the disc
 left the magazine.

 The nominal radial clearance is only 2, so line sensing must earn its place through a physical test. If the array cannot
 achieve repeatable centring, keep this architecture and add a downward camera above the output; do not pretend that
 a single colour sensor is enough.

  2. SealBot stages both beams. The input flap remains raised and locked. SealBot places the first beam at its final
     wall contact and releases the second on its marked line with a doorway left open.

  3. CareBot delivers the ten kits. It visits H, PCC-A, and PCC-B, aligns the selected chute outlet inside the zone, and
     opens the matching gate.

  4. SealBot collects samples. It lowers the input flap, enters through the reserved doorway, collects each disc, lifts it
     into the magazine, and confirms that the queue advanced.

  5. CareBot collects and sorts patients. It follows the twelve allowed starting positions, lifts one patient at a time,
     reads the colour, and fills the four destination bins.

  6. SealBot releases samples. It visits the three laboratory slots, centres the rear output, and cycles the escapement

  7. SealBot closes quarantine. From outside the boundary, its low fixed bumper slides the staged second beam into

  8. CareBot empties patient bins. It releases red at H, green at RZ, and the two yellow bins at their assigned PCCs. It

  9. Stop early enough. Both robots stop starting new work when the remaining time is less than the tested operation
     time plus parking margin. All outputs stop by 118 seconds.

 This is the full architecture sequence, not a claim that every action fits in two minutes. Batch storage reduces long
 return trips, but twelve patient pickups, three sample pickups, two beam placements, and seven destination releases
 still require timing on a full field. The competition routine may enable only the actions that pass both reliability and time
 tests.


 7. Control and sensing
 CareBot's ESP32-CAM is the main controller. It runs vision, navigation, the mission state machine, colour decisions,
 safety timeouts, and every actuator command. A PCA9685 generates the nine servo signals, a PCF8575 supplies
 expanded digital inputs and the elevator direction outputs, and two DRV8833 boards drive the left wheel, right wheel,
 and patient elevator motors. Store the yellow-bin toggle only after an accepted yellow classification and confirmed drop
 from the inspection cell.

 SealBot's ESP32-CAM is also its main controller. Its PCA9685 drives four servos, its PCF8575 reads the line array, flap
 sensor, queue sensors, beam switches, bumpers, and start button, and one DRV8833 drives both wheels. The disc
 escapement is allowed to cycle only when the robot is stopped, the output is aligned, and the queue sensor reports a
 disc.

 Both robots need an accessible latching stop switch that cuts motor and servo power, a fuse close to the battery, and
 separate regulated branches for logic, motors, and servos. Grounds remain common. The servo supply must handle
 the measured stall current even though software moves only one mechanism at a time. Gates and grippers should
 retain their loads mechanically when servo pulses stop.

 Use nonblocking state machines rather than long delay calls. Every motion has a timeout and at most one deliberate
 retry. A reset returns to a safe stopped state and never resumes with lost bin counts.

 12. Measurements still needed
 Before final CAD, measure the actual patient diameter and paint variation, patient mass, sample diameter and
 thickness, laboratory slot diameter and surface pattern, both beam lengths and thicknesses, field-line reflectance,
 start-zone tape thickness, wheel diameter, loaded current, servo travel, and the slope at which each piece starts
 sliding. Confirm the Senior randomisation rules and the exact recovery-zone boundary with the organiser.

 The architecture is now fixed: CareBot uses a roller lift, colour cell, selector, tilted destination bins, and separate tilted
 kit chutes; SealBot uses raised-flap beam grippers, a JCB-style sample loader, a gravity magazine, and a one-disc
 escapement. The prototypes should change clearances and linkage geometry, not return to the removed pocket,
 direct-delivery, caterpillar, or under-tray concepts.


 13. ESP32-CAM as the main controller
 Use one AI-Thinker-style ESP32-CAM with an OV2640 and working PSRAM on each robot. There is no Nano in this
 version. The ESP32-CAM makes the decisions and sends commands to the expansion boards; the PCA9685 and
 PCF8575 are interfaces, not secondary brains.

 The camera already consumes most ESP32 pins. With microSD disabled, use I2C to recover the channels needed by
 this architecture. A candidate mapping is GPIO13 for SDA, GPIO14 for SCL, and GPIO12, GPIO15, GPIO1, and
 GPIO3 for the four DRV8833 wheel inputs. Use GPIO2 and GPIO4 for wheel-encoder inputs only after checking the
 exact board, flash LED circuit, and boot behaviour. Add 10-kilohm pull-downs to every motor input, especially the
 boot-strapping pins. Disconnect the USB-UART programmer during a match.

 What each camera does
 CareBot's camera faces forward and slightly downward. It finds patient stickers and cylinders, follows or reacquires
 field lines, and checks destination approaches. The enclosed TCS34725 reads patient colour because fixed distance
 and fixed lighting give a cleaner measurement than asking the main camera to classify a small painted top while the
 robot moves.

 SealBot's camera sees the JCB flap and the floor ahead. It finds the 56-diameter sample outline, centres the bucket for
 pickup, watches beam approaches, and provides coarse laboratory navigation. The rear five-channel line array handles
 the last alignment over a laboratory slot because the disc outlet is behind the camera view.

 During a match, capture small frames such as QVGA and process only the required region. Do not stream video over
 Wi-Fi while driving. One task captures and reduces the image, while the control loop keeps checking motor deadlines,
 bumpers, and the 118-second stop deadline. A stale image cannot trigger pickup or release.

 Control chain
 The electrical control path is simple: camera and mission code run on the ESP32-CAM; direct GPIO drives the wheel
 motor bridge; I2C commands the servo board, digital I/O board, and CareBot colour sensor; the physical stop switch
 cuts actuator power regardless of software state.

 Fit 100 nF directly across every brushed motor, 470 microfarads at each motor-driver rail, 1000 microfarads at each
 servo board, and 470 microfarads near each ESP32-CAM. Use at least 20 AWG for the battery and regulator feeds, 22
 AWG for motor and servo branches, and thinner wire only for logic signals. Breadboards and Dupont jumpers are for
 bench signals, not motor or servo current.

 The proposed batteries are 7.4 V, 2200 mAh, 30C 2S LiPo packs with balance connectors. Charge them only with a
 compatible 2S balance charger, on a non-flammable surface, under supervision, and store them in a LiPo-safe bag. A
 cheaper protected Li-ion pack is acceptable only when its documented continuous discharge rating exceeds the
 measured robot peak and its charger matches the pack connector and chemistry.

 18. Firmware architecture
 CareBot runs BOOT_SAFE, SELF_TEST, WAIT_START, DELIVER_KITS, FIND_PATIENT, APPROACH_PATIENT,
 LIFT_PATIENT, READ_COLOUR, SELECT_BIN, DROP_TO_BIN, NEXT_PATIENT, DELIVER_PATIENT_BINS,
 PARK, and FAULT states. The camera never opens a gate directly. It returns a measurement to the mission state,
 which checks freshness, robot stop state, presence sensors, and the destination before acting.

 SealBot runs BOOT_SAFE, SELF_TEST, WAIT_START, STAGE_BEAM_A, STAGE_BEAM_B, FIND_SAMPLE,
 LOAD_SAMPLE, VERIFY_QUEUE, APPROACH_LAB, ALIGN_SLOT, RELEASE_ONE, VERIFY_OUTPUT,


CareBot / SealBot | Locked mechanism architecture | 6 September 2026                                              17
 CLOSE_QUARANTINE, PARK, and FAULT states. The disc-release state cannot run unless the queue reports a disc
 and the laboratory alignment has been accepted from a fresh stopped reading.

 Use ESP-NOW only for collision coordination between the two robots. Each robot keeps its own match clock and
 mission. If peer messages fail, both robots stop before the shared crossing instead of waiting forever or driving through
 each other.

 Store calibration in flash: camera exposure, field thresholds, line-array polarity, wheel distance per encoder count,
 turning scale, motor minimum PWM, TCS34725 colour ranges, servo pulse endpoints, mechanism timeouts,
 destination poses, and the tested worst-case duration of every mission block. A missing or invalid calibration keeps the
 robot in SELF_TEST.


 19. What to do next
  1. Order one proof set before the full cart. Buy one ESP32-CAM, programmer, PCA9685, PCF8575, DRV8833,
     TCS34725, two SG90s, one MG90S, and one line array. Prove the control stack and supplier quality before buying
     multiples.

  2. Build two bench mechanisms. Make the CareBot roller channel and SealBot three-disc gravity magazine from
     scrap. These two object paths decide whether the architecture works.

  3. Make the 480 x 280 start gauge. Put full-size beam and CareBot cardboard volumes inside it. Do this before
     accepting the stock chassis deck dimensions.

  4. Bring up one complete power tree. Set every regulator with no load, then test with a current-limited supply, one
     drive motor, and a deliberately moving servo. The ESP32-CAM must not reset.

  5. Write the shared controller foundation. Implement safe boot, PCA output disable, I2C device checks, motor stop,
     encoder counting, the 118-second deadline, and fault logging before writing mission code.

  6. Qualify sensing on real pieces. Capture patient colour readings and camera images under the venue-like lighting,
     then test the laboratory sensor over every slot.

  7. Build the remaining bins, chutes, and grippers. Freeze their dimensions only after the two risky mechanisms
     and start envelope pass.

  8. Run full-field timed trials. Record successful pieces and time for each mission block. Enable only the sequence
     that finishes with ten seconds of measured margin.

 The immediate next move is the proof set and two scrap mechanisms. Buying the entire cart before those tests risks
 spending Rs 20,000 around a roller gap or disc escapement that needs a different geometry.
